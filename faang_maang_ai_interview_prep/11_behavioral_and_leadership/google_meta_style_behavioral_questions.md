# Google/Meta-Style Behavioral Questions

> **Type:** Study notes

> **Varies:** Exact rubric names, round count, and emphasis vary by team, level, and year at both
> companies — the general shape below (structured competency scoring rather than a company-wide
> fixed list like Amazon's LPs) is well-established.

## How this differs from Amazon's approach

Google and Meta don't publish a fixed list like Amazon's 16 LPs. Instead, interviewers score
against internal competency rubrics — commonly covering things like collaboration/teamwork,
communication, handling ambiguity, technical leadership, and (at Google specifically) historically
"Googleyness" — roughly: comfort with ambiguity, collaborative instinct, intellectual humility.
Practically, this means: **fewer named buckets to prep against, but each answer needs to show
depth and self-awareness rather than checking a keyword box.**

## Representative question categories

- **Collaboration / cross-functional work**: "Tell me about a time you worked with a
  difficult stakeholder or a team with different priorities than yours."
- **Ambiguity**: "Tell me about a project where the requirements weren't clear when you
  started."
- **Technical leadership without formal authority**: "Tell me about a time you drove a
  technical decision across a team you didn't manage."
- **Growth / self-awareness**: "What's the biggest piece of feedback you've received, and what
  did you do with it?"
- **Prioritization under constraint**: "Tell me about a time you had to cut scope to hit a
  deadline. How did you decide what to cut?"

## What separates a strong answer here from an Amazon-style answer

Amazon rewards a **crisp LP-labeled story**. Google/Meta rubrics reward more **reflective depth**
— they're more likely to follow up with "what would you do differently" or "how did the other
person perceive that" than an Amazon interviewer moving to the next LP. Prepare a genuine second
layer for each story: not just what happened, but what you'd change, and how you know it worked
(what evidence, not just your own assessment).

## Model answer (illustrative)

**Question:** "Tell me about a time requirements were unclear."

> "We were asked to 'improve document processing reliability' with no specific target — a vague
> mandate typical of an early-stage internal tooling ask. Rather than guessing at scope, I spent
> the first two days pulling failure logs and categorizing the actual failure modes we had (OCR
> timeouts, malformed PDFs, and a smaller bucket of storage write failures), then went back to the
> stakeholder with three concrete options ranked by effort and impact, and got explicit agreement
> on scope before writing code. In hindsight, I'd have looped in the on-call team a day earlier —
> they had failure data I didn't know existed and it would have saved the two days of log-mining."

Note the self-critique at the end — that's the layer Google/Meta-style rubrics are specifically
listening for.
